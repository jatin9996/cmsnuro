Most back and neck pain improves without an operation. Rest for a day or two, then ordinary movement, and the right medical care, settle a large share of mechanical pain. Surgery is a tool for a smaller group: a nerve or the spinal cord that is compressed, a spine that is unstable, and selected fractures, infections, and tumours. This article explains how that distinction is made. It is general education from a neurosurgical clinic. It does not decide whether you should have an operation.

If you cannot pass urine, if urine or stool is leaking without control, if both legs are weak, or if the skin between the legs has gone numb, go to an emergency department. Those are signs of pressure on the nerves at the bottom of the spinal canal. They are not a problem for a clinic appointment later in the week.

## The spine is a column with a purpose

The spine holds you upright and protects the spinal cord and the nerve roots that leave it. Bones called vertebrae stack from the neck to the sacrum. Discs sit between the bones and work as cushions. Joints and ligaments guide movement. Muscles do most of the day-to-day work of lifting and turning.

The spinal cord runs inside the canal and ends in the upper lumbar region in adults. Below that, nerve roots travel in a bundle that is often compared to a horse’s tail. Pressure on the cord and pressure on a single nerve root are different illnesses. They look different, they are urgent to different degrees, and they are not treated with the same sentence.

A painful back is usually a problem of the discs, joints, or muscles. A painful leg or arm that follows a nerve, a hand that has become clumsy, or a walk that has become unsteady may be a problem of the nerve or the cord. The consultation exists to tell those apart.

## Neck pain and low-back pain are not the same conversation

The neck carries the cord as well as the nerves to the arms. A disc or a bony narrowing in the neck can press the cord. People then notice clumsy hands, a feeling of walking on cotton, unsteadiness, or, later, a change in bladder control. Neck pain alone, without those signs, is often a joint or muscle problem and is not an indication for surgery.

The low back, in adults, has nerve roots rather than the spinal cord. A disc there typically causes sciatica: pain down one leg, sometimes with tingling or weakness in a matching pattern. Both can be serious. The emergencies and the operations are not interchangeable, which is why a friend who “had the same pain and had a fusion” is not a template for you.

## Three patterns that should not be mixed up

Mechanical pain is worse with certain postures and better with others. It may follow a long day, a poor chair, or an awkward lift. It sits in the back or the neck more than in the limb. It is the common pattern, and it is the pattern least likely to need an operation.

Radicular pain follows a nerve. In the leg it may run past the knee into the foot. In the arm it may run past the elbow into the hand. Coughing or straining can jab it. Numbness and weakness, when they match the same nerve, support the story. Many people with a disc herniation and this pain improve over weeks without surgery. An operation is discussed when the pain remains disabling after a fair trial of non-surgical care, or when weakness is progressing.

Myelopathy is the pattern of a cord that is being squeezed. It is more often a neck problem. The hands slow down. Buttons and writing suffer. The legs feel stiff or unreliable. The person may look as if they have a balance problem rather than a pain problem, and pain can be mild. This pattern is not watched casually. Some people remain stable. Some slowly worsen. The decision about surgery is made from the examination and the images together, because waiting for the person to become unable to walk is not a kind plan.

## A disc herniation, in plain language

A disc has a firmer outer ring and a softer centre. A tear can let material press a nearby nerve. On a report this may be called a bulge, a protrusion, or an extrusion. Those words describe shape. They do not, by themselves, say that you need an operation. Large extrusions sometimes shrink on later scans. Small bulges are extremely common in people with no pain at all, especially after the age of forty.

The useful question is whether the picture matches the person. A disc on the left, at the level that serves the left foot, in a person with left-foot pain and a weak ankle, is a coherent story. A disc mentioned on a scan done “just to see,” in a person whose pain is across the low back and whose examination is normal, is often an incidental line. Operating on an incidental line does not treat the pain and adds the risks of surgery.

Sudden weakness changes the pace. A foot that slaps the floor, a new inability to stand on the toes or the heel, or a hand that can no longer grip are reasons to be seen promptly, not at the end of a long course of painkillers. Recovery of strength is less predictable once the weakness has been profound and prolonged.

## Narrowing of the canal

Stenosis means the canal, or the side opening where a nerve leaves, has less room. Bone spurs, thickened ligaments, and bulging discs all contribute. In the low back the typical story is discomfort in the legs on walking, eased by sitting or by bending forward over a cart. Standing tall makes it worse. This is called neurogenic claudication. It can be confused with poor circulation, in which the legs also hurt on walking. The distinction matters, because the treatments are different. A doctor looks at the pulses, the examination, and, when needed, both the spine and the blood vessels.

In the neck, stenosis can press the cord and produce the clumsy-hand, unsteady-walk pattern. Pain may not be the main complaint. An older person who has “slowed down” and whose family thinks it is age deserves an examination before that explanation is accepted.

Surgery for stenosis is considered when walking or hand function is clearly limited, non-surgical care has been given a real trial where that is safe, and the images show a compression that explains the story. The operation is usually a decompression: making room. A fusion is added only when the spine also needs to be stabilised, not as a routine companion to every decompression.

## A slip of one bone on another

Spondylolisthesis means one vertebra has moved forward or backward on the one below it. Some people are born with a predisposition. Some develop it as joints wear. Some slips follow a fracture of the pars, the small bridge of bone at the back of the vertebra. Many slips are mild and need no operation. A slip becomes a surgical conversation when it is unstable, when it presses nerves, or when pain and function remain poor despite proper non-surgical care.

Not every slip needs a fusion, and not every fusion is done for a slip. The word on the report is the start of the discussion. Flexion and extension X-rays are sometimes used to see whether the slip moves. An MRI shows the nerves. The decision uses both, plus the examination.

## Fractures

A fracture after a high-energy injury, such as a road crash or a fall from a height, is an emergency until a doctor has confirmed that the cord and the alignment are safe. Do not sit the person up or remove a collar that has been applied at the scene unless the treating team says so.

A fracture after a minor fall, a bout of coughing, or no remembered injury at all raises a different concern, especially in an older person, in someone on long-term steroids, or in someone with a known cancer. The bone may be weak. Some of these fractures are treated with pain control, a brace, and treatment of the bone health. Some, particularly when the bone has pushed into the canal or the person is neurologically worse, need an operation. Vertebroplasty and kyphoplasty are procedures used in selected painful fractures. They are not a treatment for every old collapse seen on an X-ray, and they are not decided from a blog.

## Infection and tumour

Fever, a wound on the back, an infection elsewhere, intravenous drug use, dialysis, or a suppressed immune system can accompany a spinal infection. Night pain that is relentless, unexplained weight loss, and a personal history of cancer are reasons to image sooner rather than to stretch physiotherapy over months. An abscess pressing the cord or the cauda equina is urgent. Tuberculosis of the spine is still seen in India and can present with pain, a deformity, or a slow deficit. It is a medical and sometimes a surgical illness. It is not something to diagnose at home from a night sweat.

A past cancer does not mean the present pain is a metastasis. It does mean the pain should not be filed under “ordinary strain” without a thought. Tell the doctor about the cancer even if it was years ago and you consider it finished.

## Cauda equina compression

The nerves that control the bladder, the bowel, and the saddle area can be pressed by a large disc, a clot, an abscess, or a tumour. The warning picture is more than sciatica.

- Numbness in the saddle area, the region that would sit on a bicycle seat
- Inability to pass urine, or urine that leaks without warning
- Loss of bowel control
- Weakness in both legs, or weakness that is advancing
- Sciatica in both legs, especially if it is new

One of these, in the setting of back pain, is enough to go to an emergency department. Do not wait to see if the morning is better. Do not take a sleeping tablet and review it tomorrow. Recovery of bladder function is time-sensitive. An operation, when it is required, is done to remove the pressure. Delay is the part a person can still influence by arriving early.

## Red flags that should move you up the queue

Ask for a same-day medical review, and use emergency services if the deficit is sudden or severe, when any of the following is true.

- A new and definite weakness, a foot drop, or a hand that has lost grip
- Numbness that is spreading, or numbness in the saddle area
- Any change in bladder or bowel control
- Back or neck pain with fever
- Pain after a significant injury
- Pain in a person with cancer, unexplained weight loss, or long-term steroid use
- Pain that is worse at night and is not eased by rest, when this is a new pattern
- Unsteadiness or clumsy hands that are new
- Pain in a child, which is not “just a strain” until a doctor has said so

Ordinary pain after a long day, without these features, can be assessed in a planned way. The list is there so that the dangerous minority is not treated with the advice meant for the majority.

## What “tried non-surgical care” actually means

For a stable radicular pain without progressive weakness, a period of proper non-surgical care is reasonable. That period is measured in weeks, not in a single afternoon, and not in years of worsening function. It may include medicines prescribed for you, guided physiotherapy, and a change in how you sit, lift, and sleep. It may include an injection for selected nerve pain, done by someone who does that work, after the images have been seen.

It does not mean staying in bed. Prolonged bed rest weakens muscle and often worsens the next month. It does not mean forcing a painful twist because a video said to. It does not mean borrowing a relative’s pain tablets, muscle relaxant, or steroid. Doses are individual, and some of those drugs are unsafe with blood thinners, ulcers, kidney disease, or glaucoma.

If weakness is increasing while you are “giving it time,” the time is over. Come back.

## When an operation becomes the sensible option

A surgeon recommends an operation when three things line up. The symptoms and the examination show a nerve or the cord under pressure, or a spine that is unstable. The images show a cause that fits that examination, at the right level and on the right side. The expected benefit, for pain, strength, walking, or bladder function, is greater than the risk of that particular operation in that particular person.

Pain that is severe but does not match the scan is not solved by operating on the scan. A scan that looks dramatic in a person who is comfortable and neurologically well is not an order to operate this week. The aim is function: walking, hand use, bladder control, and a life that is not dominated by nerve pain. A normal-looking X-ray is not the aim.

## Operations, named in ordinary words

A discectomy removes the piece of disc that is pressing a nerve. It does not replace the whole disc as a matter of routine. A decompression, sometimes called a laminectomy or a laminotomy, removes bone and ligament to give a squeezed nerve or cord more room. A fusion joins two or more bones so that a painful or unstable segment stops moving. Screws and rods are one way to hold that join while bone heals. They are not required for every back operation, and they are not a mark of a more serious surgeon. An artificial disc is an option in highly selected necks and, less often, low backs. It is not a general upgrade.

Surgery for a spinal tumour may be done to remove pressure, to stabilise a bone that is about to collapse, or to obtain a diagnosis. The cancer treatment, if the tumour is a metastasis, continues with oncology. Surgery for infection may drain pus and stabilise a destroyed bone, alongside the long course of antibiotics that an infection specialist directs.

The approach, from the back, from the front of the neck, or through a smaller tube, is chosen for the anatomy. A smaller incision is not automatically a smaller operation, and a microscope or a navigation system is a tool, not a guarantee. Ask what the operation is trying to protect, what will be left in place, and what the alternative is if you do not have it.

## Risks that should be said out loud

Bleeding, infection, a spinal-fluid leak, and a wound that is slow to heal are risks of spine surgery in general. So is a failure to relieve the pain, especially when the pain was never clearly from the compressed nerve. New weakness, numbness, or a change in bladder control can occur, and in the neck the cord itself is at stake. Adjacent segments can wear later after a fusion. A join can fail to fuse, particularly in people who smoke or who have poor bone. Anaesthetic risk depends on the heart, the lungs, and fitness, which is why those histories are not small talk.

The percentage you want is the risk for your operation, your level, and your health. A number quoted from a different procedure is not yours. Smoking, poorly controlled diabetes, and continuing a blood thinner against advice all move the risk in the wrong direction. Stopping a prescribed blood thinner is also dangerous. Only the doctor who started it, in discussion with the surgeon, should change it.

## Scans, and the words they use

An MRI is the usual picture when a nerve or the cord is the question and the person is stable. It shows discs, nerves, and soft tissue. A CT shows bone in detail and is used for fractures, for planning, and when an MRI cannot be done. X-rays show alignment and, when taken bending forward and back, can show a slip that moves. They do not show a disc pressing a nerve.

You do not need an MRI for every first week of back pain without red flags. You do need a prompt image when the red flags in this article are present. Bring the actual files to the visit. A report that says “disc bulge at L4–L5” without the pictures leaves the surgeon unable to see side, size, or whether the nerve is truly contacted.

Other phrases deserve a translation. Annular tear means a split in the outer ring. Facet arthropathy means wear in the small joints at the back of the spine. Ligamentum flavum hypertrophy means a ligament has thickened and is taking up room. Modic change means the bone next to a disc looks altered on MRI. Myelomalacia means the cord itself shows signal change, which is a sign of injury, not a casual adjective. None of these words is an instruction. All of them are reasons to ask what, in this scan, is actually touching a nerve.

## What the consultation is for

Bring the timeline, the medicine list, previous operations, and every disc. Say what you cannot do: the distance you can walk, the nights you cannot sleep, the work you have stopped. A family member who has watched the walking is useful. The examination tests power, feeling, reflexes, and gait. In the neck it looks for signs of cord irritation. In the low back it looks for a nerve-root pattern and for saddle sensation and anal tone when the story raises cauda equina. Those examinations are clinical. Do not try to reproduce them at home.

You should leave with one of a few plans. An emergency admission, if the findings demand it. A non-surgical plan with a clear review date. Further images. Or a recommendation for an operation, with the reason, the alternative, and the risks stated in sentences you can repeat to your family. If you leave with only the name of an implant, ask for the reason again.

## Recovery is a plan, not a slogan

After a decompression or a discectomy, many people walk on the first day, with limits that the surgeon sets. Pain at the wound is expected. Increasing leg or arm pain, a new weakness, fever, a wound that drains, or a new bladder problem is not expected and should be reported. A fusion has a longer protection of the bone join. Lifting, bending, and return to two-wheelers or to heavy work are timed to that healing, not to a neighbour’s story.

Physiotherapy after surgery is about walking, confidence, and the muscles that will carry you. It is not a race to touch your toes. Smoking slows fusion and wound healing. Good diabetes control and enough protein help. The tablet list for home, especially for nerve pain or for a blood thinner if one was prescribed, should be written down. Stopping or doubling those tablets on your own is a common way to spoil a good operation.

Return to work depends on the work. A desk may be possible sooner than a building site. Ask for the restriction in writing so that an employer is not inventing one. Driving waits until you could safely make an emergency stop and you are no longer using medicines that cloud reaction time. Your surgeon, not this page, sets that date.

## Daily life while you are deciding

Walk as much as the pain and the safety of your legs allow. Change position. Avoid the one posture that clearly jabs a nerve, without taking to bed. Do not lift a load by bending and twisting at the same time. A firm, simple chair is usually kinder than a deep soft sofa. Heat or a short period of relative rest can take the edge off muscle pain. They do not decompress a cord.

Keep a two-line diary: how far you walked today, and whether any weakness or bladder symptom appeared. That diary is more useful in the clinic than a pain score remembered from last month. If you are waiting for a planned operation and a new saddle numbness or a bladder change appears, the wait is cancelled. Go to emergency care and tell them you were listed for a spine operation.

## Myths that push people both ways

A slipped disc is not a bone that has popped out and must be pushed back by hand. Manipulation of an acute, weak, or cord-compressed spine can injure it. “Age-related, so nothing can be done” is equally wrong. Age changes risk. It does not forbid an operation that would protect walking. A normal vitamin D level will not pull a disc off a nerve, and a low vitamin D level is still worth treating for bone health. A belt does not stabilise an unstable slip. Traction at home is not a treatment for cord compression. A scan done three years ago does not describe the leg that became weak this week.

The opposite myth is that an operation is modern and therefore better. For ordinary mechanical back pain without a compressive or unstable target, surgery is often the worse choice. The skilful recommendation is sometimes the one that says no.

## Questions worth taking into the room

Ask which nerve or which part of the cord is compressed, and at which level. Ask what happens to people like you over the next few months without an operation. Ask what the operation is intended to improve, and what it is unlikely to improve, such as long-standing numbness or a worn joint elsewhere. Ask whether a fusion is required and why. Ask about the risks that apply to your heart, your diabetes, your smoking, and your bone. Ask what symptom, while you wait, should send you to emergency care instead of back to the clinic. Ask when you would walk, when you would drive, and when you would return to your actual job.

A second opinion is reasonable before a fusion, before a decision you do not understand, or when the pain and the report do not seem to describe the same person. Take the same images. A verbal recollection of a scan is a poor substitute for the pictures.

## Questions people ask

**I have had an MRI and it mentions a disc. Do I need surgery?**

Not on that sentence alone. Disc findings are common in people without symptoms. Surgery depends on a matching examination, a failure of proper non-surgical care or a progressive deficit, and a target that an operation can sensibly treat.

**How long should I wait with sciatica?**

If strength is stable and the bladder and saddle area are normal, a number of weeks of guided care is a standard path, and many people improve in that time. The exact length is set by your doctor. Increasing weakness, or any bladder or saddle change, ends the wait.

**Is a fusion a more complete operation?**

A fusion solves instability or a problem that decompression alone would leave painful or unsafe. Added to a decompression that did not need it, a fusion adds operating time, a join that must heal, and effects on the neighbouring levels. Completeness is doing what the spine in front of you requires.

**Can physiotherapy make a disc worse?**

Movement that is guided, after a serious cause has been considered, is part of care. Forcing range through a progressive weakness, or manipulating a neck that shows cord compression, is different. Tell the physiotherapist about numbness, weakness, and any bladder change, and stop and seek a medical review if those appear or worsen.

**The pain has gone but the foot is still weak. Is that success?**

Pain and power recover on different clocks. Improving pain with a residual foot drop still needs a review of whether the nerve is free and whether a brace or further treatment would help function. Do not assume silence of the pain means the nerve has recovered.

**Can I use a two-wheeler?**

Not if your legs are weak, your neck is in a brace, or your reaction time is slowed by pain or by tablets. After surgery the surgeon sets the date. Until then, a fall on a two-wheeler is a poor way to test a spine that is already injured.

**What if I am afraid of surgery and the cord is compressed?**

Say so. Fear is a reason for a clearer explanation and a second opinion. It is not a reason to disappear. Untreated cord compression can cost walking and hand function that an operation later cannot fully return. The choice should be informed, and it should be made while there is still function to protect.

**Do injections replace an operation?**

For some nerve-root pain, a carefully chosen injection reduces pain while a disc settles. It does not stabilise a slip, it does not decompress a tight cord as a substitute for surgery when surgery is indicated, and it should not be repeated endlessly in the face of a worsening deficit.

## A clear close

Most people with back or neck pain will never need a spine operation, and they should not be talked into one. A smaller group will be harmed by waiting: the person with cauda equina compression, the person whose hands and walking are failing from a squeezed cord, the person with a fracture, an abscess, or a tumour in a dangerous place. The work of a consultation is to put you in the right group.

Bring the images and a plain account of what you can no longer do. NEUROKALP can offer that planned opinion. If the bladder, the saddle area, or both legs have changed today, do not come to the clinic first. Go to an emergency department.

## The neck, looked at more closely

The cervical cord is the part of the nervous system that a neck problem can injure permanently. People rarely describe it as cord compression. They say the fingers feel thick, that coins are hard to pick up, that the feet do not know where the step is, or that a walk across a dark room has become uncertain. Neck pain may be absent. Headache at the back of the head can be present and can be blamed on screens.

Handwriting that has become larger and slower, a shirt that can no longer be buttoned, and a spoon that falls are practical tests you can notice at home. They are not a diagnosis. They are a reason to be examined before another year of “age” is added to the story. Once walking needs a stick that was not needed a season ago, the conversation has already become late for some people, and still worth having.

An operation on the neck, when it is advised, is usually intended to stop further loss and to give the cord room. Feeling and walking may improve. They do not always return to the way they were five years earlier, especially if the cord already shows injury on the scan. That limit is a reason to decide while function remains, not a reason to refuse a discussion.

The approach may be from the front of the neck or from the back. From the front, a disc is often removed and the height restored with a graft or a cage, sometimes with a plate. From the back, bone and ligament are removed to enlarge the canal, and a fusion is added when the neck would be unstable without it. Hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, and a sore neck are discussed with the front approach. Neck pain and the healing of the muscles are discussed with the back approach. Which one is safer depends on where the pressure sits, how many levels are involved, and the shape of the neck. It is not a preference you need to arrive with.

## The low back, looked at more closely

Sciatica from a lower lumbar disc often travels below the knee. Buttock pain that stops at the thigh can still be a joint. The distinction is clinical, and a scan that shows a disc at the same time does not settle it by itself. Pins and needles in the sole, a weak push-off from the toes, and a reduced reflex are pieces of a nerve pattern. Your doctor maps them. You do not need to learn the map to be a good historian. You need to say which toes feel different and whether the heel or the toe is hard to lift.

A large central disc can press more than one root and can threaten the cauda equina even when the pain is not the worst pain of your life. The bladder question is part of every serious back assessment for that reason. Answer it literally. Going more often, straining, leaking, or a feeling that the bladder is not empty all count as changes. “I think it is the prostate” or “I think it is the prostate medicine” may be true, and it still needs to be said out loud so that it is not assumed.

Lateral pain in the hip and thigh, worse on lying on that side, is often a tendon or a joint rather than a disc. Groin pain can be the hip joint. A spine operation does not replace a worn hip, and a hip operation does not decompress a nerve. When both are worn, the examination decides which one explains today’s limitation. Say which movement stops you: walking distance, stairs, or getting up from a chair.

## Bone, smoking, and the conditions that change the risk

Soft bone does not forbid surgery, and it changes the hold of any screws and the chance of a further collapse. A recent fracture from a minor fall, a loss of height, a long course of steroids, and an early menopause are worth mentioning. Treatment of the bone is part of care whether or not an operation happens. It is prescribed. It is not a substitute for decompressing a cord.

Smoking tightens the odds against a fusion and slows wounds. Stopping, even a few weeks before a planned operation, is useful. Continuing up to the morning of surgery and assuming the hardware will compensate is not. Diabetes that swings from high to low raises infection risk. Bring the readings rather than the word “controlled.” Blood thinners, including those bought as supplements that affect clotting, belong on the list. Turmeric in food is not the issue. Unprescribed high-dose products and borrowed tablets are.

Weight changes the effort of surgery and of rehabilitation. It is not a moral category, and it is not a reason to ignore a progressive deficit until a target weight is reached. A cord that is failing should not wait for a season of dieting. A purely painful problem with a long non-surgical path may be a place where fitness helps the next decision. Ask which of those two situations is yours.

## Children, adolescents, and older adults

A child with back pain needs an examination. Strain exists, and so do infection, a slip in a growing spine, and other causes that are uncommon in adults and unwise to assume away. Night pain, fever, a limp, a change in the shape of the back, and pain that stops play are reasons to be seen promptly. This page is not a paediatric clinic. It is a warning against using adult advice on a child.

Adolescents who train hard can have a stress injury of the pars, the bridge of bone at the back of the vertebra. It may heal with rest from the provoking sport. It is not a reason for an immediate fusion, and it is not a reason to keep bowling or tumbling through pain because a tournament is near. A doctor and, when needed, images set the restriction.

Older adults are often told that their scan is “all worn” and that nothing can be done. Wear is common. A clear stenosis that has taken the ability to walk to the market is not the same as a scan that looks old in a person who still walks well. Age adds anaesthetic risk and adds the value of protecting independence. Both belong in the conversation. A blanket refusal, or a blanket enthusiasm for screws, is not an assessment.

## Pain that is not a surgical target

Widespread pain, profound tiredness, a scan that does not match, and a story of many operations elsewhere should slow everyone down. Operating again because the last operation disappointed is how people accumulate scars without a function gained. A pain specialist, a physician, and sometimes a psychiatrist or a psychologist are part of competent spine care. Recommending them is not a dismissal. It is a recognition that the nervous system can amplify pain after the structural problem has been addressed, and that some pain never had a structural target.

Compensation claims, workplace disputes, and family conflict do not cause a disc. They do complicate recovery, and an honest consultation names them without accusing the person of inventing a deficit. Weakness that is present on examination is a finding. Pain that is out of proportion to a modest scan is also a finding. The plan has to fit both.

## Preparing for a planned operation

You will be asked about the heart, the lungs, teeth that are infected, skin sores, and any fever in the days before. An infected tooth or a boil on the back can postpone an operation that involves metal. Say so early. Plan the home you will return to: a toilet that is reachable, a chair with arms, medicines you already take, and a person who can stay the first nights. Stop only the tablets you were told to stop, on the day you were told to stop them.

On the day, the side and the level should be marked and confirmed with you. Ask what “success” will feel like at six weeks, and what would count as a complication you should not sit on at home. Fever, a wound that opens or drains, a new weakness, a severe headache that is worse when upright, and any bladder change are the usual reasons to call. A headache that is better when lying flat can be a sign of a spinal-fluid leak and should be reported, not treated with a weekend of painkillers alone.

Walking after surgery is encouraged because it reduces clots in the legs and keeps the lungs clear. It is not a test of character. If the legs are weaker than they were before the anaesthetic wore off, say so. Pain at the incision is expected. Pain that is a new, sharp nerve pain, different from the pain you came in with, should be described clearly so that it is not folded into “post-operative pain” without a look.

## The months after

A decompression for a pinched nerve often declares itself over days to weeks: less leg pain first, power next, numbness last and sometimes never complete. A fusion is judged over months, because bone has to heal. X-rays in that period look at position and, later, at the join. They are not a monthly MRI. Activity opens up in stages that your surgeon sets. Comparing your week-two walk with a cousin’s week-two walk after a different operation is how people either despair or overdo.

Return to a two-wheeler, to lifting a child, and to a job site are separate permissions. Get each one. Sexual activity is usually limited at first by the wound and by comfort, not by a universal ban. Ask, particularly after a fusion, what bending and strain to avoid. Physiotherapy should know the operation that was done. A generic back class that twists a fresh fusion is not the same as a programme built for you.

If pain returns months later, the possibilities include a recurrent disc at the same level, a problem at the next level, a join that did not heal, and ordinary muscle pain. The way back is an examination and, when indicated, a new image. It is not an automatic second operation, and it is not something to stretch for a year if a new weakness has appeared.

## Work, sport, and the story you tell yourself

A job that involves lifting, vibration, or a helmet and a long ride needs a specific restriction, not “light duty” as a vague phrase. Give the surgeon the real task. “I lift sacks” and “I sit at a desk” produce different letters. Sport can return. Impact and collision wait longer than walking and swimming. A young person with a healing pars injury who returns for a final match is a person choosing a fracture over a season. Say that trade out loud before you make it.

People delay surgery because they are afraid, and people have surgery because they are tired of being the patient in the family. Neither emotion is a clinical indication. The indication remains the nerve, the cord, the stability, and the life that is being lost to a problem an operation can actually change. A good consultation will say no when the emotion is running ahead of the images, and will say do not wait when the cord is running out of time.

## More questions people ask

**The MRI was done lying down and my pain is only when I stand. Has the scan missed it?**

It can. Stenosis and some slips declare themselves in extension and in standing. That is why the history and, sometimes, standing X-rays or a second look at the images matter. Describe the posture that brings the pain on, and the posture that relieves it, before anyone offers an operation or a clean bill of health.

**I am pain free on tablets. Why discuss surgery?**

If the tablets are controlling a stable nerve pain and the examination is sound, surgery may not be needed. If the tablets are hiding a progressing weakness, or if they cannot be taken safely because of the kidneys, the stomach, or the heart, the calculation changes. Bring the list and the side effects, not only the pain score.

**Can I use a belt and avoid the operation?**

A belt can remind you not to bend and can comfort a muscle pain. It does not open a tight canal and it does not protect a cord. Use it if it helps and a clinician has not told you it is delaying a needed assessment. Do not use it as a reason to skip the visit.

**What is the difference between a neurosurgeon and an orthopaedic spine surgeon for this problem?**

Both train to operate on the spine. The right surgeon is one who looks after the condition you actually have, who will examine you, and who will say when you do not need an operation. For cord compression, cauda equina, spinal tumours, and problems that sit beside the nervous system, a neurosurgical opinion is a natural fit. The emergency department is still the first door for the emergencies named above.

**Will I be paralysed if I do not operate this month?**

Sometimes the honest answer is that the risk of further loss is high, particularly with cord compression that is already causing clumsiness and imbalance, or with cauda equina compression today. Sometimes the honest answer is that a painful disc with stable power is likely to settle, and paralysis is the wrong fear. Ask for the answer that belongs to your scan. This page cannot give it.

**Is endoscopic or laser surgery always better?**

A smaller corridor is an advantage when the target is a contained disc and the anatomy suits it. It is not an advantage when the canal is tight over several levels, when the spine needs a fusion, or when the diagnosis is a tumour or an infection. The tool does not make the indication. Ask what would be removed, what would be left, and what the surgeon would do if the smaller approach could not finish the job safely.

## The decision, stated once more

Have an operation when a compressed nerve or cord, an unstable spine, or a selected fracture, infection, or tumour is the thing limiting your life, and when the images and the examination agree. Do not have an operation because a report mentioned a bulge, because a relative had screws, or because pain has been present for a week and the internet is frightening. Do not refuse an operation out of fear when the bladder is involved or the hands and the walking are slipping away.

Write down the limitation, bring the pictures, and ask what will happen if you wait. The answer to that question is the consultation. Everything else is a detail that should hang from it.

## The thoracic spine is easy to overlook

Between the neck and the low back, the thoracic cord is protected by the rib cage and is injured less often. When it is injured, the symptoms are easy to misfile. People describe a band of tightness around the chest, numb legs, and walking that has become stiff. They may have been treated for a gastric problem, a heart problem, or asthma because the band feels internal. Pain between the shoulder blades at night, especially with a history of cancer, infection, or a minor fall in soft bone, deserves an examination that includes the legs and the walking.

An MRI of the lumbar spine does not picture the thoracic cord. If the legs are weak or numb and the low-back scan does not explain it, the next image may need to be higher. Say “both legs” and “a band around the chest” clearly. A consultation that only looks at the lumbar report will miss what was never photographed.

## Instability, in ordinary language

A spine is unstable when its bones can shift in a way that threatens the cord, the nerves, or the ability to stand without severe pain. This is a mechanical fact, seen on standing films, on bending films, or on a scan after injury. It is not a feeling of being fragile, and it is not a word a report uses for ordinary wear.

After a fracture or a dislocation, instability can be immediate. The collar or the brace stays on until a surgeon has decided. After a slip that moves on bending films, instability can be a reason to discuss fusion rather than decompression alone. After an infection or a tumour has destroyed a vertebral body, the bone in front may no longer carry load. Stabilisation is then part of protecting the cord, alongside the treatment of the infection or the cancer.

Feeling “loose” after a long day is not instability. Needing to push on the thighs to stand up is often muscle and hip. The films answer the structural question. The history answers how much it matters.

## Revision surgery

A second operation is sometimes the right operation: a recurrent disc at the same site, a stenosis that was not fully addressed, a join that did not heal, or an adjacent level that has later compressed a nerve. It is sometimes the wrong operation, offered because the first one did not cure a pain that was never from that disc. The threshold should be higher, not lower, the second time. Scars make the nerves harder to protect. The indication has to be cleaner.

Bring the first operation note and the images from before and after it. “They put screws” is not an operation note. The level, the year, and what was removed change the plan. If those papers are in another city, ask that hospital for them before you travel for an opinion. A fresh MRI still helps. It does not replace the knowledge of what was done.

## Clots, wounds, and the early danger signs

A clot in the leg can follow any period in bed, and spine surgery is one of those periods. A calf that swells, that is warm and tender, or a sudden breathlessness and chest pain, is a reason for urgent care. Moving the ankles and walking as advised are prevention. They are not a treatment for a calf that has already swollen.

A wound that is dry and a little pink is healing. A wound that is increasingly red, that smells, that opens, or that drains pus needs a review, particularly if metal was used or if the person is diabetic. A clear watery leak from the wound can be spinal fluid and should be reported. So should a headache that is bad when sitting and relieved by lying flat. Fever is not a normal part of the second week.

New saddle numbness or a new inability to pass urine after surgery is an emergency even if you have just been discharged and the clinic is closed. Go to the hospital that operated, or to an emergency department if you are far away, and say when the operation was and what has changed.

## Sleep, chairs, and the working day

A pillow that keeps the neck in a middle position is usually kinder than a stack that pushes the head forward. On the side, a pillow between the knees can ease a low back. On the stomach, the neck is twisted for hours. Many people with neck or back pain do better if they leave that position. These are comforts, not treatments for a compressed cord.

A chair that supports the low back, with the feet reaching the floor, beats a deep seat that rounds the spine. Standing up between long meetings is reasonable. A standing desk does not decompress a stenosis and can worsen leg symptoms in people whose pain comes on with extension. Change position. Do not turn one posture into a moral good.

Lifting a child, a gas cylinder, or a suitcase by bending at the waist and twisting is the movement most likely to provoke a disc that is already symptomatic. Hold the load close, use the legs, and ask for help with the loads that are simply too heavy. This advice is for prevention and for recovery. It will not push a sequestered disc off a nerve that has already lost power.

## Sport and fitness without making things worse

Walking is the default exercise while a decision is being made, if the legs are safe and the pain allows. Swimming or a stationary cycle suits some people with stenosis because the spine is flexed. It suits them less if the shoulder or the neck is the problem. A physiotherapist who has seen the report can choose. A video that promises to “pop the disc back” cannot.

Weights, bowling, wrestling, and contact sport wait until a clinician has said the bone and the nerve can take them. A healing fracture, a fresh fusion, and a cord that is compressed are three different reasons to stop, and the return dates are different. Pain that shoots down the leg during an exercise is a signal to stop that exercise. Muscle ache the next day is a different signal. Learn the difference, and do not brave the shooting pain.

## How to take a second opinion without losing the plot

Carry the same disc to both surgeons. Write the first recommendation in a sentence: “Decompression at this level, no fusion, after six weeks if the foot remains weak.” Ask the second surgeon whether they agree with the target, the timing, and the need for a fusion. Disagreement about the implant brand is less important than disagreement about whether to operate at all.

Do not collect opinions until the emergency has passed. Cauda equina, a worsening cord, and an abscess are not second-opinion conditions for next week. They are treatment conditions for today. A second opinion is a privilege of a stable problem.

If the two opinions differ, ask each what fact would change their mind. Sometimes the missing fact is a bending X-ray, a fresh examination of power, or a period of proper physiotherapy that has not yet been tried. Fill the gap. Do not average the opinions into an operation that neither of them quite recommended.

## Words on a spine report

**Disc bulge.** A broad, shallow extension of disc. Common, and often painless.

**Protrusion.** A more focal disc extension, still attached.

**Extrusion.** Disc material that has pushed further out, sometimes still in contact with the parent disc.

**Sequestration.** A fragment that has separated. It can be very painful, and it can also shrink with time. Weakness and bladder signs still set the urgency.

**Nerve-root compression.** Contact and flattening of a named root. It matters most when the side and the level match the symptoms.

**Central canal stenosis.** Narrowing of the main canal. In the neck this threatens the cord. In the low back it threatens the roots.

**Foraminal stenosis.** Narrowing of the side exit of a single nerve.

**Ligamentum flavum thickening.** A ligament at the back of the canal taking up space. Often part of stenosis.

**Facet arthropathy.** Wear of the small joints behind the canal. A common cause of mechanical pain, and a contributor to narrowing.

**Spondylolisthesis.** One vertebra slipped on the next.

**Spondylolysis.** A defect in the pars, the bony bridge, which can allow a slip.

**Myelomalacia.** Signal change inside the cord, suggesting the cord itself has been injured.

**Modic change.** Altered signal in the bone next to a disc. It explains some pain. It is not an indication for surgery by itself.

**Annular tear.** A split in the outer ring of the disc. It can hurt. It does not mean the disc must be removed.

**Thecal sac.** The membrane-lined tube that contains the roots and the fluid. Reports describe how indented it is.

**Claudication.** Leg pain on walking. Neurogenic claudication comes from the canal and eases with sitting. Vascular claudication comes from blood vessels. They are separated by an examination, not by the word alone.

## Before you decide

Read the red flags once more on the day you make the decision. A new bladder change, new saddle numbness, or a foot that has become weaker since the last visit changes the decision. Call, or go to emergency care, rather than keeping an outpatient date that was made for a stable pain.

If none of those has happened, the decision can be calm. Some of the best spine operations are the ones that are not done, because the disc settled and the person returned to work. Some of the most costly delays are the weeks spent on a cord that was already declaring itself in the hands and the gait. A careful opinion is the ability to tell those weeks apart.

Bring the images to NEUROKALP if you want that opinion in a planned visit. Bring yourself to an emergency department if the emergencies on this page are happening now. The two doors are different, and using the right one is part of the treatment.

## A visit, from the waiting room to the decision

Arrive with the images already copied, not with a promise that someone will forward them. Arrive with a list of medicines written in block letters, including tablets bought without a prescription. Wear clothing that lets the back, the neck, and the legs be examined without a struggle. A companion who has seen you walk is worth more than a second folder of internet printouts.

The history should take the time it takes. Start with what you cannot do this month that you could do three months ago. Then give the first day the pain or the weakness appeared, any injury, any cancer, any fever, and any change in the bladder or bowel. Mention the neck if the problem is the legs, and the legs if the problem is the neck. The cord connects them.

The examination is not a courtesy. Power is graded. Feeling is compared. Reflexes are tapped. You will be asked to walk, to stand on the heels and the toes if it is safe, and, when the story demands it, to answer direct questions about the saddle area. These questions are clinical. Answer them without embarrassment. A missed cauda equina starts, too often, with a question that was softened until it disappeared.

The images are then matched to that examination. You should be able to see, on the screen or on the film, the level the surgeon is talking about. If the words stay abstract, ask them to point. A decision you cannot picture is a decision you cannot weigh.

At the end, ask for the plan in one of four forms. Emergency treatment. A non-surgical programme and a review date. Another test. Or an operation, with the goal, the alternative, and the risks. Repeat it back. The sentence you repeat is the sentence you will actually remember in the car.

## If you are the person who will be at home afterwards

Learn the wound before you leave the hospital, so that a later change is obvious. Know who to call at night. Know which hospital has the records. Stock the ordinary medicines and the prescribed ones before the day of surgery, so that the first evening is not spent searching a closed chemist. Clear the path from the bed to the toilet. A rug that has been harmless for ten years is a rug that catches a foot in a brace.

Do not add a herbal anti-inflammatory, a high-dose painkiller, or a blood-thinning supplement because the prescribed tablets seem slow. Some of them increase bleeding. Some of them injure the kidney that the prescribed painkiller is also asking to work. Tell the surgeon what you plan to take. Then take what was agreed.

Encourage walking as advised. Discourage heroics. The first fortnight is for healing and for noticing complications, not for proving that the operation was small. A temperature, a leaking wound, a new weakness, a calf that swells, chest pain, or a bladder that will not empty is a call the same day. Pride is a poor dressing.

## Work letters, families, and the long argument

Employers need restrictions they can follow. “No lifting more than the limit your surgeon sets, no riding a two-wheeler until cleared, review on this date.” A letter that says “rest” invites the workplace to invent the rest of it. Ask for specifics that match the job you actually do.

Families divide, sometimes, into those who want an operation immediately and those who are afraid of one. You cannot settle that argument on a messaging group. You can ask both sides to hear the same sentence from the same surgeon: what happens if we wait, and what is put at risk if we operate. People calm down when the risk is named. They argue when the risk is a rumour.

Children in the house should be told that a back or a neck is injured, that an operation is or is not planned, and that they did not cause it by being carried or by asking to play. They should not be asked to interpret a scan. If a parent’s walking is changing quickly, another adult has to be in charge of the lifting. A compressed cord is not a reason to keep carrying a child until the date on the appointment card.

## What this page owes you

It owes you a clear line between ordinary pain and dangerous pressure. Most of you will be on the ordinary side, and you should be protected from an operation you do not need. Some of you will be on the dangerous side, and you should be protected from a delay dressed up as courage.

It does not owe you a diagnosis. It cannot see your scan, test your power, or tell you which level is guilty. That is the consultation. Bring the facts, ask what waiting costs, and use the emergency door when the bladder, the saddle area, both legs, or a rapidly failing cord will not wait for it.

## A checklist before you leave any clinic

You should be able to say which level of the spine is under discussion, and whether the cord, a single nerve, or neither is compressed.

You should be able to say whether the plan is to wait, to start or continue non-surgical care, to arrange a further image, or to operate.

You should know the symptom that cancels the wait. For the low back that symptom includes saddle numbness and a bladder or bowel that has changed. For the neck it includes worsening hands or walking. For either, it includes fever after a recent infection concern, or pain after a new injury.

You should have the images in your possession, not only a line on a report. You should have the medicine changes in writing, especially if a blood thinner is to be stopped or restarted.

You should know the review date. A plan without a date is a way of losing people who are quietly getting worse.

If any of those sentences is missing, ask before you stand up. The corridor is where clear plans turn into family arguments. The room is where they can still be repaired.

## Ordinary pain, one last time

Wake with a stiff back, move gently, and let the day loosen it. Change your chair. Walk. Sleep enough. Lift loads close to the body. If the pain is mechanical, these unglamorous steps are the treatment, and they are more effective than an operation aimed at a disc that was never the cause.

Do not frighten yourself with a scan done for reassurance and then read every bulge as a verdict. Ask a clinician whether the finding matches the pain. If it does not, treat the person in front of the scan, not the sentence on the paper.

Do not let pride keep you from the emergency door. The person who cannot pass urine, whose saddle area is numb, whose hands are failing, or whose legs are both giving way is not being brave by finishing the day at work. They are spending function they may not get back.

NEUROKALP can sit with you for the planned decision, look at the images, and say whether surgery belongs in your story. The emergency services and the hospital emergency department sit at the other door, and for the situations named on this page that door is the correct one. Using it is not an overreaction. It is the treatment.

## Situations families recognise

The person who has had a week of low-back pain after lifting a suitcase, who is otherwise well, who can feel the saddle area, and who passes urine normally, is the person this page does not want to see in an operating theatre. They need time, movement, and a doctor if the pain is severe. They do not need screws because a relative had them.

The person whose pain runs down one leg to the foot, who is slowly improving, and whose ankle is as strong as last week, is the person for whom a fair trial of non-surgical care is often right. They need a review date so that “fair” does not become “forgotten.”

The person whose foot has started to slap the ground this week is no longer in that trial. Strength is leaving. They need a prompt examination and a scan that can be acted on, not another course of tablets started over the telephone.

The person who cannot pass urine since last night, or who cannot feel the toilet seat, is in an emergency. The hour matters. The clinic appointment does not.

The older person whose hands have become clumsy and whose family says the walk has shrunk to the courtyard is the person whose neck may be squeezing the cord. Pain may be the smallest part of the story. An examination is the next step, not a collar bought from a shop.

The person with fever and a back that is worse at night, or with a known cancer and a new band of chest tightness and heavy legs, is the person for whom “wait and do physiotherapy” can be a serious error. Tell the doctor about the fever and the cancer in the first minute.

The person who has already had one operation and is being offered a second because the report still mentions a disc needs the old notes and a slower conversation. Sometimes the second operation is exactly right. Sometimes it is a scar in search of a reason. The match between weakness and the picture decides.

The person who is simply frightened by a word on a report, and whose examination is normal, needs an explanation and a plan for the finding. They may need nothing else. Reassurance that is based on the images is part of treatment. Reassurance that has not looked at the images is a guess.

## What you can do this week if you are stable

Walk on level ground if the legs are trustworthy. Stop if a nerve pain shoots or a foot gives way. Change position often. Lift only what you can hold close. Sleep with the neck in a middle position. Take only the medicines prescribed for you. Write one line a day on distance walked and on whether the bladder and the strength are the same.

Do not start a steroid because a neighbour’s disc was treated with one. Do not hang from a bar. Do not let anyone forcefully twist a neck that has not been examined. Do not drive a two-wheeler if the foot is weak or the neck is not under control.

If any red flag appears during this quiet week, the quiet week is over. That sentence is the one to remember when the rest of the page has blurred.

## The sentence to leave with the family

Most back and neck pain does not need an operation, and should not be given one to satisfy a scan.

An operation is for a nerve or a cord that is compressed, a spine that is unstable, or a selected fracture, infection, or tumour.

Saddle numbness, a bladder or bowel that has changed, both legs failing, or hands and walking that are slipping away are not waiting-room problems.

Bring the images. Ask what happens if you wait. Leave with a date or an emergency plan, not with a vague instruction to rest.

NEUROKALP can make that planned judgement with you. The emergency department is where you go when the judgement cannot wait until the next clinic day.

Say the bladder symptoms without softening them.

Say which foot is weak, and since which day.

Say whether the hands have changed, even if the pain is in the back.

Bring the disc, not a photograph of the report.

Ask what waiting costs.

Ask what the operation is for.

Ask what would make tonight an emergency.

Then follow the answer you were given, including the answer that says you do not need surgery.

Pain alone is not an operation.

A matching, progressive deficit can be.

The bladder does not wait for a convenient day.

Neither does a cord that is already changing the hands and the walk.
