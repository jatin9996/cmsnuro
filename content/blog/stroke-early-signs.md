A stroke is a sudden injury to the brain caused by a blocked or a burst blood vessel. It is an emergency. If the face droops, an arm or a leg becomes weak, speech slurs, vision drops, or balance fails all at once, call emergency services. In India the numbers are 112 and 108. Do not wait for a clinic appointment, do not finish a meal, and do not lie down to see whether it passes.

This page is general education. It cannot tell you whether the person in front of you is having a stroke. If you are unsure, treat it as a stroke. Arriving early at a hospital that can scan the brain is safer than arriving late with a correct guess.

## What is happening

Brain tissue depends on a continuous supply of blood. A clot or a severe narrowing can block a vessel. That is an ischaemic stroke, the more common kind. A vessel can also tear or burst, so that blood leaks into the brain or into the space around it. That is a haemorrhagic stroke. Both can kill tissue. Both can look the same at the bedside. Only a scan separates them, which is why no one at home should decide the treatment.

A transient ischaemic attack, often called a mini-stroke, is a short-lived version of the same blockage. The signs may fade in minutes. The fading is not a reason to relax. A transient attack is a warning that a larger stroke may follow, sometimes within hours or days. It belongs in an emergency department the same day, even if the person is talking normally again by the time you are reading this.

## The signs that should make you call

Use a simple check, and use it once. Do not repeat it for an hour to be sure.

**Face.** Ask the person to smile. One side may hang. The mouth may pull to one side. Saliva may spill. The eyelid may not close properly.

**Arm.** Ask them to hold both arms out, palms up, with the eyes closed. One arm may drift down. A leg may be too weak to stand on. The grip on one side may be useless. The weakness is usually one-sided, not a general tiredness.

**Speech.** Ask them to repeat an ordinary sentence. The words may be slurred, the wrong words may come, or they may not understand the request at all. Some people are silent. Some speak fluently and say nothing that makes sense.

**Time.** Note the time you last knew they were well, and call. Treatment decisions in hospital are counted from that moment, not from the moment you decided to worry.

Two further signs belong in the same call.

**Balance.** A sudden inability to walk, a veer to one side, or a room that spins, especially with other signs, can be a stroke in the back part of the brain. People are often told it is an ear problem. Sudden unsteadiness with vomiting, double vision, or a new headache should not be left at home.

**Eyes.** Sudden loss of vision in one eye, loss of one half of the visual world, or double vision that has just appeared, needs the same urgent path. A curtain coming down over one eye can be a warning from the circulation to that eye and to the brain.

A sudden, worst-ever headache, particularly if it is followed by vomiting, a stiff neck, collapse, or confusion, can be a bleed. That is also an emergency. It is not a migraine to sleep off until a doctor who knows the person has said so.

## What not to do while you wait

Do not give food, water, or tea. Swallowing can fail during a stroke, and a sip can go into the lungs.

Do not give aspirin, a blood thinner, a blood-pressure tablet, or any other medicine unless the emergency team tells you to. Aspirin helps some blocked-vessel strokes later. It can worsen a bleed. You cannot tell which this is without a scan.

Do not give insulin, sugar, or a sleeping tablet as an experiment. Low blood sugar can mimic a stroke, and the hospital will check it. Home treatment delays the scan.

Do not drive yourself. A companion may drive if an ambulance cannot reach you and a hospital is close, while someone else calls ahead. The person with the signs sits in the back, watched, with the time of onset written down.

Do not stop at the clinic, the family doctor, or a scanning centre that cannot treat a stroke. The first stop is a hospital emergency department. A planned neurosurgical opinion is for later, when the emergency has been treated and a question about recovery, a vessel, or a bleed remains.

Do not restrain the person roughly if a seizure occurs. Keep them on their side if you can do so safely, and do not put anything in the mouth. A seizure can accompany a stroke. It does not change the need to call.

## Why the first hours are different from the next week

When a vessel blocks, an area of brain may already be injured and a surrounding area may still be salvageable if blood flow returns. Hospitals that treat stroke can, in selected people and inside a strict time window, give a clot-dissolving medicine or remove a clot with a catheter. Those treatments are decided after a scan, a check for bleeding risk, and a clock. They are not tablets to keep at home. Arriving inside the window is the part the family controls.

A bleed is the opposite problem. The team works to limit further bleeding, to control pressure, and to decide whether an operation, a coiling procedure, or intensive medical care is the right next step. None of that can start in a living room.

Write down three facts before you leave the house, if you can do it without delaying the call.

- The time the person was last seen well, even if that was when they went to bed
- The medicines they take, especially blood thinners, insulin, and blood-pressure tablets
- Allergies, and whether they have had a recent operation, a known cancer, or a previous bleed

“Last seen well” matters more than “when we noticed.” A person who went to sleep normal and woke with a deficit has a last-seen-well time of the night before. Say that plainly. Do not round it to a more convenient hour.

## Signs that are easy to explain away

A stroke is not always a dramatic fall. An older person may simply be “not making sense” at breakfast. A hand may be called clumsy. A droop may be blamed on tiredness or on a dental injection. A young person may be told it is anxiety because strokes are thought of as a disease of the old. Strokes happen in young adults, in pregnancy, and after the puerperium. Age changes the likelihood. It does not cancel the signs.

Low blood sugar, a seizure, a severe migraine, an inner-ear crisis, and a high or low sodium can all imitate a stroke. That list is why the emergency department exists. It is not a list of reasons to stay home and try a sweet drink first. If the person is awake enough to drink and you already know they are hypoglycaemic because this is their usual episode, follow the plan their own doctor has given. If you are guessing, call.

Bell’s palsy weakens the face, often including the forehead, and usually spares the arm and the speech. A stroke more often spares the forehead and may involve the arm or speech. Families should not be asked to make that distinction under pressure. Face weakness that is new is a reason to be examined the same day. If the arm, the leg, or the speech is also involved, it is an emergency call.

A migraine aura can cause flashing lights, tingling, or trouble finding words, usually followed by a familiar headache in a person who has had it before. A first episode, an aura that is different, weakness that stays, or an older person with vascular disease should not be filed under migraine without an examination. When the signs are sudden and one-sided, call.

## What the hospital is doing

The first job is to protect the airway, the breathing, and the circulation, and to measure glucose. A CT scan is often done at once because it shows a bleed quickly. MRI is used when it will change the decision. The team asks the clock, the medicines, and the last time the person was normal. Blood tests look at clotting, counts, and chemistry.

If the scan shows a blockage and the person fits the criteria, a stroke physician may offer a clot-dissolving drug, or a radiologist may remove a clot. If the scan shows a bleed, those drugs are not used. A neurosurgeon may be asked to see a person with a large bleed, a bleed in the cerebellum, a blocked fluid pathway, or an aneurysm that has ruptured. Many bleeds are cared for without an operation. The recommendation is individual. It is not a rule you can apply from the size mentioned in a report over the telephone.

Blood pressure will be managed. The target is not “as low as possible” in the first hours, and home readings should not be chased with extra tablets on the way to hospital. Bring the usual medicines. Let the team decide.

## After the immediate danger

The days that follow are about the cause and about function. A blocked vessel may have come from a narrowing in the neck, from an irregular heart rhythm such as atrial fibrillation, from a small-vessel disease related to blood pressure, or from less common clotting problems. Tests can include a heart tracing, a longer heart monitor, an ultrasound of the neck vessels, an echocardiogram, and blood tests. A bleed may lead to vessel imaging to look for an aneurysm, a malformation, or a tumour that bled. Not every person needs every test. The pattern chooses them.

Medicines to reduce the chance of another stroke are prescribed after the type is known. They may include a medicine against platelets, a medicine to thin the blood, a medicine for blood pressure, and a medicine for cholesterol. The choice depends on the cause, the bleed risk, the kidneys, and the other drugs already in use. Starting a relative’s blood thinner, or restarting one that was stopped for a procedure, without asking, is a common and serious mistake. So is stopping a prescribed one because a neighbour said it is dangerous. Ask the team that knows the scan.

Rehabilitation starts early. Physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech and swallowing therapy are not extras for later if the deficit is significant. Sitting out of bed, when it is safe, protects the lungs and the skin. A person who coughs on water needs a swallowing assessment before the family brings favourite food from home.

## Recovery is uneven, and it is real

Some deficits improve in the first days as swelling settles. Some improve over months as other areas of the brain take on work and as muscles are retrained. Some do not fully return. No article can say which group a person will be in. Early improvement does not mean the medicines can stop. A plateau does not mean effort is pointless.

Watch for a new deficit after an initial improvement. A person who was speaking and then is not, or who develops a sudden headache and drowsiness in the ward or at home, needs an urgent reassessment. Swelling, a second stroke, a seizure, and bleeding into an injured area are all possible. Families are often the first to notice. Say what changed and when.

Spasticity, shoulder pain on a weak side, constipation, low mood, and fatigue are common in the weeks after. They deserve treatment. They are not a sign that the family is failing. Depression after a stroke is an illness, not a lack of gratitude. Mention it at the review.

## Lowering the chance of another stroke

The plan is individual, and it is made by the physicians who have the results. The themes are well known. Treat blood pressure rather than hoping the next reading will be better. Treat diabetes without swinging into low sugar. Stop smoking. If atrial fibrillation has been found, understand why a blood thinner was or was not chosen. Move every day within the limits the therapists set. Limit alcohol. Sleep, and ask about snoring and witnessed pauses if those are part of the story. These steps support the medicines. They do not replace them, and they do not replace the emergency call if a new sign appears.

A carotid narrowing, when it is the cause and when it is severe, is sometimes treated with an operation or a stent. That decision weighs the narrowing, the recent symptoms, the heart, and the surgical risk. It is not offered for every irregularity mentioned on an ultrasound. Bring the images to that discussion.

## Living at home after a stroke

Make the house kinder before a fall teaches the lesson. A rail, a chair in the bathing area, shoes that hold the foot, and a clear path at night matter more than a new gadget. A weak arm should be positioned as the therapist showed, so the shoulder is not left hanging. A foot that drops may need a brace. Practise the exercises that were actually prescribed. Adding a vigorous routine from the internet can injure a shoulder or exhaust a person who is still in the early weeks.

Driving is not automatic once the person feels confident. Weakness, a visual-field loss, a seizure, and slowed thinking all affect the road. Ask the treating doctor for a clear yes, no, or not yet. Until that answer, do not drive and do not ride a two-wheeler.

Work and worship and travel can return. The timing depends on the deficit, the cause, and whether the medicines are stable. Air travel after a recent stroke should be discussed, not assumed. Carry a list of the drugs and a short note of the diagnosis. Do not leave the blood-pressure or blood-thinner tablets in a checked bag.

## For the family in the first week

Choose one person to speak to the medical team so the story stays accurate. Ask what type of stroke it was, what the immediate risks are, and which change should bring you back to the desk at night. Ask when swallowing was assessed. Ask the names of the medicines and what each is for. Write them down. You will not remember them in the corridor.

Children in the house need a true, short explanation. A parent or a grandparent is ill, the doctors are treating the brain, and the person may speak slowly or not use one arm. They do not need every scan result. They do need to know they did not cause it.

If the person was alone when it happened, do not spend the next day on blame. Use it to plan the next time: a phone within reach, a neighbour who has a key, a morning call. Guilt does not restore the hours that were lost. A plan can save the next ones.

## Myths

A stroke is not a heart attack. The two can share causes, and a heart rhythm can cause a stroke, but the emergency is in the brain. Pain in the chest with sweating and breathlessness is its own emergency. Both are reasons to call. Neither is a reason to choose which hospital service you prefer and drive past the other.

Mustard oil, a squeezed finger, a pasted herb, or a cold bath does not open a blocked brain vessel. Waiting for a traditional remedy is a way to miss the treatment window. Prayer and family courage are compatible with calling an ambulance. They are not a substitute for it.

A normal blood-pressure reading at home does not rule out a stroke. Pressure can be high, normal, or low during one. A person who has never had high pressure can still have a clot from the heart or a bleed from a vessel malformation.

Improvement in the car is not proof that it was “just gas” or “just fatigue.” Transient signs still need the same-day assessment described above.

## Questions people ask

**The signs lasted ten minutes and have gone. Can we come to the clinic tomorrow?**

No. Come to an emergency department today. A transient attack is a warning stroke. The clinic can help with later questions. It cannot offer the scan and the monitoring that this afternoon requires.

**Should I give aspirin on the way?**

No. Aspirin is a hospital decision after a scan has shown that this is not a bleed, and after the other medicines have been checked. A single home dose is a common error.

**The person is elderly and would not want a ventilator. Should we still call?**

Yes. Many strokes are treated without a ventilator. Calling does not force a treatment the person would refuse. It allows a scan, comfort, and a decision made with information. Wishes about intensive care can be told to the team on arrival.

**Can stress or a quarrel cause a stroke?**

A quarrel does not create a clot by itself. Very high blood pressure, a burst vessel, and a heart rhythm can declare themselves at a moment of strain. The family quarrel is not the diagnosis, and it should not delay the call.

**Is a stroke always painful?**

No. Many blocked-vessel strokes are painless. Sudden headache is more a feature of some bleeds and of some posterior strokes. Absence of pain is not reassurance.

**What if it happens at night and we only notice in the morning?**

Call then. Tell the team the time the person was last definitely normal, and the time you found them. Some treatments depend on that interval. Others, including care of a bleed, blood pressure, swallowing, and rehabilitation, still start immediately. Do not wait for the clinic to open.

**Can a neurosurgeon stop the stroke?**

A neurosurgeon is involved when a bleed, a blocked fluid space, a vessel malformation, or a selected neck vessel needs a surgical opinion. Most ischaemic strokes are led by stroke physicians. The first door is still the emergency department, not a neurosurgical appointment book.

**After we are home, which new event means we call again?**

Any new face droop, weakness, speech change, sudden vision loss, sudden severe headache, a seizure, vomiting with drowsiness, a wound or a headache after a procedure, or a fall with a head injury while on a blood thinner. A mild, familiar tiredness can be mentioned at the planned review. A new one-sided sign cannot.

## A clear close

Learn the signs before you need them. Face, arm, speech, time, and also sudden loss of balance or vision. Call 112 or 108. Do not give food or aspirin. Do not stop at a clinic on the way to a hospital. After the emergency, follow the prevention plan that was built for that person, and treat any new sign as a new emergency.

## How a stroke can look in ordinary rooms

At the breakfast table the spoon may fall from one hand. The person may miss the cup on one side, or turn the head because one eye seems not to be working. Speech may be slightly thick, enough that a relative asks them to repeat and then decides it is sleep. If the change is sudden and one-sided, it is a call, not a second cup of tea.

On the telephone the voice may be the only clue. Ask them to smile and to lift both arms even though you cannot see, and ask someone nearby to look. If they cannot complete a sentence, or they sound newly confused, stay on the line only long enough to tell a neighbour or the emergency service the address. Do not spend the call persuading them that they are fine.

In a meeting or a shop the person may sit down, say they are dizzy, and ask for a minute. Give them the minute only in the sense of making them safe. A sudden lean to one side, a dropped object, or words that will not form is the moment to call. Embarrassment is not an injury. A missed window is.

At night you may hear a fall, a strange breath, or nothing at all until morning. Do not spend time reconstructing which hour would have been more convenient. Note when you last had a normal conversation, and call. If they are on the floor, keep them warm, do not drag them upright if a neck injury from the fall is possible, and wait for help unless the place is unsafe.

During pregnancy and in the weeks after delivery, a sudden headache, a seizure, a visual change, or one-sided weakness is an emergency. Blood pressure diseases of pregnancy can injure the brain. So can clots. The obstetric team and the emergency team need to see the person. A clinic visit the next afternoon is the wrong door.

In a younger adult the same signs are sometimes called a panic attack because a stroke feels unlikely. Panic can cause tingling around the mouth and in both hands, a racing heart, and a fear of dying, usually without a one-sided face, a drifted arm, or lost words. When the signs are one-sided or the speech has broken, do not use age as a reassurance. Call.

## What to say when you call

Give the address first, including a landmark. Say that you suspect a stroke. Give the time last seen well. Say whether the person is awake, breathing, and able to talk. Mention a fall, a seizure, pregnancy, or a blood-thinner tablet if you know it. Do not give a long medical history before the address. The history can follow once help is coming.

If you are travelling to hospital by car because an ambulance cannot reach you, call the emergency department or have another person do it, and say you are bringing a suspected stroke and the time of onset. One person drives. One person watches. The person who is unwell does not sit alone in the back with a bottle of water “in case.”

Bring the medicine strips, a previous discharge summary, and the identification the hospital will ask for. Leave the rest of the luggage. A scan is more useful than a change of clothes in the first hour. Clothes can come later.

## Two kinds of emergency that share one door

A blocked vessel and a burst vessel both need a hospital, and they need opposite medicines. This is why the journey matters more than the family’s theory. A person with a known clotting problem, a mechanical heart valve, or a recent clot in the leg is still not a person you should anticoagulate at home. A person with a known aneurysm is still not a person whose headache you should medicate and observe overnight.

A subarachnoid haemorrhage, bleeding into the fluid around the brain, often starts as a headache that patients later call the worst of their life, sometimes during effort, sometimes at rest. The neck may stiffen. Vomiting and brief loss of consciousness are common. Some people remain awake and are told they look too well for a serious illness. Sudden worst headache is enough. Call.

A bleed inside the brain tissue may cause a sudden deficit and a headache, and the person may become drowsy as pressure rises. A blocked vessel may cause a deficit with no headache at all. You will not sort these in the car. The scan will.

## Words you will hear after the scan

Ischaemic means too little blood. Haemorrhagic means bleeding. A territory such as the middle cerebral artery is a map of which functions sit in that supply: face and arm, language on the usual dominant side, and part of vision. A lacunar stroke is a small deep injury, often linked to long-standing blood pressure. Small does not mean trivial if it has taken walking or swallowing.

Aphasia is a language problem. The person may not find words, may not understand, or may both. Intelligence has not left. Speak in short sentences, one idea at a time, and do not shout. Dysarthria is a slur from weak muscles of speech. The language is intact and the mouth will not shape it. Neglect is a stranger problem: the person may not notice the left side of the body or the room. Putting food only on the side they notice, and approaching from that side, is practical. Scolding them for “ignoring” you is not.

Penumbra is a word for brain that is struggling and may still be saved. Core is a word for tissue already injured. Families sometimes hear these in a corridor and think they are grades of blame. They are descriptions of a scan at a moment in time.

Thrombolysis means a clot-dissolving medicine given in hospital, in a limited number of hours, and only after a bleed has been excluded. Thrombectomy means removal of a clot with a catheter in selected blockages of larger vessels, sometimes a little later than the medicine window, still on a clock. Both are decisions. Neither is a request a family can insist on if the scan, the time, or the bleeding risk says no. Asking what was considered, and why it was or was not offered, is reasonable the next day. It should not be a quarrel in the CT room.

## The first days in hospital

Swallowing is checked before a meal. A wet voice, a cough, or a temperature after drinking is a reason to stop and tell the nurse. A chest infection from a silent aspiration can set recovery back more than the original weakness. Honour the texture that was advised. Mashing a favourite meal at the bedside because the person looks hungry is a loving way to cause harm.

Blood pressure will be watched. The first-day numbers are often allowed to sit higher than the long-term goal, because the injured brain needs pressure to push blood through narrowed vessels. Chasing a home target with an extra tablet from a handbag can drop the pressure too far. Ask before you give anything, including the person’s own morning dose, until the team has written the plan.

Sugar is watched for the same reason. Very high and very low readings both injure recovery. If you have been the person who manages their insulin, hand that role to the ward until discharge. Doses that were right at home can be wrong in an illness.

The weak limb needs a position that protects the shoulder and the heel. A pulled arm during a transfer can cause a pain that lasts months. Tell every new helper which side is weak. A calendar above the bed, glasses, hearing aids, and dentures are not luxuries. A person who cannot hear will be described as confused, and a person without glasses will be described as neglectful.

Ask, once a day is enough, what the night staff should be called for. New drowsiness, a worse weakness, a seizure, a severe headache, vomiting, or a fall are the usual answers. Write the name of the nurse on duty. You will forget it when you need it.

## Going home

Discharge is safer when four things are written. The diagnosis in one sentence. The medicines, with which ones are new. The follow-up date, and which symptoms cancel the wait and mean a return to emergency care. The therapy plan: who is walking with them, who is watching the swallow, and what not to attempt on the stairs.

A blood thinner and a fall risk need a practical conversation. Remove loose rugs. Put a light on the way to the toilet. Do not stop the thinner because you are afraid of a fall unless the prescribing doctor has agreed. A head injury while on a thinner is itself a reason to be checked, even if the person seems unchanged.

Constipation, a urine infection, and poor sleep can all make an older person look newly confused. They still need a medical look, because a new stroke can hide in the same picture. Do not decide it is “just the bladder” and add a leftover antibiotic.

Mood often dips in the second or third week, when the family has gone back to work and the deficit is no longer a crisis but a fact. Ask the person, and ask the person who is with them all day. Loss of interest, waking too early, and talk of being a burden are reasons to tell the doctor. They are treatable. They are not a character flaw.

Fatigue after a stroke is physical. A short visit can wipe out an afternoon. Plan the important conversation in the morning. Protect a rest. Pushing through exhaustion does not train the brain the way repetition of a specific therapy task does.

## Work, worship, fasting, and travel

Return to work is a medical and a practical decision. A desk may be possible before a job on a ladder, a night shift, or a role that requires driving. Ask for the restriction in a letter so an employer is not inventing one. Cognitive fatigue, a field defect, and a weak hand are easy for a confident person to underestimate.

Festivals and fasts matter in many households. Missing a blood-pressure tablet or a blood thinner for a day of fasting can undo months of prevention. If a fast is important, ask the physician in advance whether the timing of doses can be shifted. Do not skip the dose and announce it afterwards. A sweet, a particular oil, or a herbal drink is not a substitute for the prescribed prevention.

Air travel, a hill journey, or a long train ride should wait until the treating doctor says the recent stroke, the medicines, and the fitness to sit are compatible with it. Carry medicines in the cabin bag. Stand and move the legs as advised on a long sitting journey, especially if the stroke was related to a clot. This is general caution, not a personal clearance.

Sexual life can resume when the person feels able and the doctor has not set a restriction, often after the blood pressure and the medicines are stable. Fear is common. A sudden severe headache or chest pain during exertion is a reason to stop and seek care, as it would be in any other setting. Silence in the clinic is the usual reason this question goes unanswered. Ask it.

## For the person providing care

You will be tired, and you will be frightened of the next sign. Eat, sleep, and let another relative take a shift. A exhausted caregiver misses the very change they are watching for. Learn the therapy tasks so that practice happens on ordinary days, and resist adding unprescribed drills that exhaust the weak shoulder.

Keep one notebook. The left-hand page is medicines and appointments. The right-hand page is new events: a fall, a missed dose, a day of slurred speech, a fever. That notebook is the consultation. Memory after a month of hospital visits is not.

If the person cannot be left alone, say so at the review. Equipment, a visiting therapist, or a different discharge plan may be available. Pretending that the household is coping, until a fall or a burn, helps no one.

## Questions that come up later

**Can they be left alone for an hour?**

Only when walking, judgement, and the way to call for help are safe. A person with neglect, a seizure risk, or a recent fall should not be testing this on the first afternoon home. Ask the therapist to watch a walk to the toilet before you decide.

**The hand is still useless after two weeks. Has recovery finished?**

No. Early change is encouraging, and later change still happens with practice and with the natural settling of the injury. A hand that is developing tightness, pain, or a shoulder that is dropping needs a review so that contracture is not added to the stroke.

**Should we repeat the MRI every month?**

Not as a routine. Imaging is repeated when the diagnosis is uncertain, when a procedure is planned, or when a new sign appears. Anxiety is a reason for a conversation. It is not, by itself, a reason for a monthly scan.

**Can a mild stroke be ignored if the person feels normal the next day?**

The feeling of normality does not retire the cause. The vessel, the heart rhythm, or the pressure that produced a brief deficit is still there until it has been looked for and treated. Same-day emergency care still applies, even when the story is already in the past tense.

**Is physiotherapy enough if we do not like the medicines?**

Therapy restores function. It does not thin the blood, open a narrowed neck vessel, or treat atrial fibrillation. Both belong in the plan when both have been recommended. Refusing a medicine is a right. It should be a discussion with the prescriber, not a silent omission.

**What do we bring to a later neurosurgical visit?**

Bring the discharge summary, the images from the acute scan and any vessel study, the medicine list, and a clear question. Common questions are about a bleed that was managed without surgery, a vessel malformation, pressure on the brain, or a neck vessel. The visit is elective. A new deficit on the morning of the appointment still means the emergency department, not the waiting room.

## What to remember

Call early. Say the time last seen well. Do not feed, do not give aspirin, and do not stop at a clinic on the way. After survival, take the prevention that was prescribed, practise the therapy that was prescribed, and treat any new one-sided sign as a new emergency. A planned opinion can wait. A stroke that is happening cannot.

## A practical script for the first hour

Stand where the person cannot fall. If they are standing, help them to sit or lie on their side before you start explaining. Unlock the door so that help can enter. Put dogs in another room. Switch on a light. These small acts feel trivial and they save minutes when the team arrives.

Say the address, then the landmark, then “suspected stroke.” Say the time. If the only time you know is “she was normal when she went to the kitchen and wrong when she came back,” say those two events. Do not invent a precise minute.

Collect the strips of medicine, not a remembered list. Blood thinners have several names. The strip is clearer than the family’s certainty. If the strips are in another house, say that you do not know, which is safer than a guess.

Keep the person nil by mouth. Wipe the mouth if saliva pools. Loosen a tight collar. Do not pour water over the head, do not force a finger into the fist, and do not slap the face to “wake the nerve.” None of those acts restores blood flow.

If they vomit, turn them onto the side if the neck has not been injured in a fall. If they had a fall from a height or a road crash, keep the neck still and let the emergency team move them. A stroke and a spine injury can arrive together. Do not choose one to treat in the hallway.

When the ambulance or the car is ready, send one calm person with the facts. The rest of the family can follow. A crowd in the resuscitation bay does not speed the scan. One person who knows the time last seen well does.

## What the team may ask, and how to answer

They will ask whether the signs are better, worse, or the same since onset. “A little better” is worth saying, and it does not mean you should have stayed home. They will ask about a previous stroke, a heart valve, a stent, a bleed in the stomach, and a recent operation. They will ask about pregnancy. They will ask the last dose of a blood thinner. “I think it is the white tablet in the evening” is less useful than the strip. If you do not know, say you do not know.

They may ask the person to smile, to lift the arms, to name a watch, and to say where they are. Do not answer for them unless they cannot speak. The point of the question is to see what the person can do. Coaching them to the right answer hides the deficit.

You may be asked to consent for a scan with dye, for a clot-dissolving medicine, or for a procedure. Ask what the scan has already shown, what the option is meant to change, and what the main risk is in the next hour. You do not need to understand the whole of stroke medicine. You need the reason and the risk in plain words. If the person was clear, earlier in the week, that they would not want intensive care, say that. It can be honoured more easily if it is said early.

## The days after, in more detail

A temperature, a rising sleepiness, or a weaker side than yesterday is a reason to call the nurse immediately, including at night. Swelling around a large stroke can peak after a day or two. A bleed can extend. A seizure can occur. Families sometimes notice the change before the next scheduled round. Use the call bell.

A physiotherapist may sit the person on the edge of the bed sooner than the family expects. That is usually deliberate. Lungs, pressure on the skin, and the sense of being upright all matter. If a drip or a urine catheter is in place, ask before you decide the person is “too tied down to move.” The staff will show you what can move.

Speech therapy is not only for people who cannot talk. Swallowing is part of the same assessment. A person who speaks clearly and coughs on water still needs the restriction. Write the recommended texture on a paper and tape it where food arrives, because a well-meaning visitor will otherwise bring the wrong meal.

Occupational therapy looks at the practical hand: a cup, a shirt, a wash. Splints and shoulder supports are used for a reason. Removing them because they look uncomfortable, without asking, can let a shoulder drop. Ask for the hours they should be worn.

A low mood in hospital is common and is not always depression. Waking in a strange room, without glasses, with a weak arm, would unsettle anyone. Persistent sadness, refusal to eat, or talk of hopelessness still needs to be passed on. So does agitation at night, which can be the room, the bladder, a medicine, or a new confusion that needs a medical look.

## Prevention, without a personal prescription

Blood pressure that is high on most home readings, taken properly after sitting quietly, is a reason to see a doctor. It is not a reason to borrow a relative’s tablet for the weekend. The dose that is safe depends on the kidneys, the other medicines, and whether a stroke has just happened. Home machines are useful when someone has taught you the cuff size and the timing. A single reading taken in anger is not a diagnosis.

Atrial fibrillation is an irregular heart rhythm that can let a clot form and travel to the brain. Some people feel palpitations. Some feel nothing, which is why a short tracing in the emergency department is not always the end of the heart investigation. If a longer monitor or an echocardiogram has been advised, do it. A blood thinner, if prescribed for this reason, prevents strokes only if it is actually taken. Missing doses because the person feels well is a common way to have a second event.

Cholesterol medicines and platelet medicines are likewise individual. Muscle pain, a black stool, a vomit of blood, or a rash after a new tablet should be reported. Stopping the whole list because one tablet caused an ache leaves the person unprotected. Ask which one to hold and which to continue.

Smoking after a stroke is not a private habit with a private consequence. It injures the lining of vessels that have already declared themselves. Help to stop is more effective than a lecture in the corridor. Alcohol in binges raises blood pressure and can trigger irregular rhythms. A daily limit, if any, should come from the physician who knows the liver and the medicines.

Movement after the acute phase, as the therapist allows, helps pressure, sugar, mood, and the strength that remains. It does not replace a blood thinner that was prescribed for a heart clot. Walk with the support that was issued. A relative who holds the weak arm by the hand and pulls is a common cause of shoulder pain. Hold the trunk or use the method you were shown.

## Life in the months that follow

The home programme is usually short and specific. Ten purposeful repetitions beat an hour of exhausted struggling. Fatigue is a signal to rest, not a failure of will. Note what time of day the arm is at its best and do the skilled task then.

Spasticity, the tightness that appears as the weeks pass, can help a weak leg stand and can also curl a hand into a fist that cannot be washed. Review it before the skin breaks down in the palm. Treatments exist. They are prescribed after an examination. Stretching that was taught in hospital should be continued. Forcing a joint past pain can injure it.

Speech may return in fragments. Friends should wait for the word rather than finish every sentence, unless the person prefers help. Group conversations are hard when the background is noisy. One speaker at a time is a reasonable request. A notebook, a phone with pictures, or a simple yes and no card can reduce isolation while language recovers. Intelligence is often intact when speech is not. Talk to the person, not about them, in their presence.

Vision that has lost a field does not repair itself because the person turns the head once or twice. Scanning practice, as taught, and a clear discussion about cooking, crossing a road, and driving, prevent burns and collisions. A person who reads only half the plate will leave half the meal. Turn the plate and sit on the side they see.

Return to the kitchen, to prayers, and to the shop is part of rehabilitation. Start with supervision. Hot oil, a two-wheeler, and a dark staircase are the three places families most often regret an early experiment. Make those three a conscious decision, not a habit that resumes because the person insists they are well.

A second opinion on prevention is reasonable if the cause was never explained, if a neck vessel was called severe, or if a bleed’s source is still unclear. Bring the images. A new one-sided weakness on the day you planned to travel for that opinion is still an emergency in the city you are standing in. Do not board a train to keep an appointment through an evolving stroke.

## Words families meet

**Ischaemic stroke.** Brain injury from a blocked or severely reduced blood supply.

**Haemorrhagic stroke.** Brain injury from bleeding into the brain or around it.

**Transient ischaemic attack.** A brief deficit from a temporary loss of flow. The signs go. The danger does not.

**Last seen well.** The last time the person was reliably normal. Treatment clocks use this time.

**Thrombolysis.** A hospital medicine that can dissolve a clot in a narrow time window after a bleed has been excluded.

**Thrombectomy.** Physical removal of a clot from a large brain vessel in selected cases, also on a clock.

**Atrial fibrillation.** An irregular rhythm that can send a clot to the brain.

**Carotid stenosis.** Narrowing of a neck artery that supplies the brain. Severe, recently symptomatic narrowing is sometimes treated. Minor irregularity is not an operation.

**Aphasia.** Loss or disruption of language, either understanding, speaking, reading, or more than one of these.

**Dysarthria.** Slurred speech from weak or uncoordinated muscles, with language itself preserved.

**Dysphagia.** Unsafe swallowing. It is a medical finding, not fussiness about food.

**Neglect.** Reduced awareness of one side of space or of the body, usually the side opposite the injured brain.

**Spasticity.** Velocity-dependent tightness of muscle that can develop weeks after the injury.

**Penumbra.** Tissue that is under-supplied and may still be salvageable at the time of the scan.

## A final word for the household

Put 112 and 108 where they will be seen. Tell every adult in the house the signs: face, arm, speech, sudden loss of vision, sudden loss of balance, sudden worst headache. Agree that any one of those signs means a call, even if the person asks to wait for a relative to return from work. The relative can meet you at the hospital.

After a stroke has been treated, keep the follow-up, keep the medicines, and keep the therapy. Bring remaining questions to a planned visit with the images in your hand. NEUROKALP can be that planned visit when the question is neurosurgical. The emergency number is the visit that cannot be booked.

## Teaching the signs to a household

Choose a quiet moment, not the middle of an illness, and spend ten minutes. Ask everyone who lives in the house, including a teenager and a driver, to name the signs without looking. Face. Arm. Speech. Time. Then add the two that people forget: a sudden loss of vision, and a sudden loss of balance, and the sudden worst headache.

Practise the sentence they will say on the phone. Address first. “Suspected stroke.” Time last seen well. Do not practise by frightening an older person who is already anxious. Do practise until the address can be said without searching for it.

Agree on the hospital before you need it. The right hospital is one that can scan the brain in an emergency and treat a stroke, not the one that is merely familiar. Ask a doctor you trust, before any illness, which emergency department they would want their own parent taken to. Write it down. In the moment, you will not want to compare reputations.

Agree that a transient recovery does not cancel the call. The person who feels better in the car is still going to hospital. This rule prevents the most common U-turn families make, and the one they most regret.

## If you are the person having the symptoms

You may feel well enough to argue. You may be embarrassed. You may not want the neighbours to see an ambulance. Those feelings are human, and they are not information about your brain. Let someone else make the call. Sit or lie down. Do not drive. Do not eat. Do not take a tablet to “settle it” unless the emergency service has told you to.

If your speech is muddled, point to your face or your arm. Keep your identification and your medicine strips where your hands usually are, so that another person can find them. If you live alone, a daily call with a relative is not a luxury after the first warning event. It is a way for someone to notice the morning you do not answer.

After you recover, you will be tempted to minimise the story so that life can resume. Tell the truth at the follow-up. A deficit that lasted minutes is still a deficit. The prevention you are offered depends on that honesty. So does the advice about driving, work, and fasting.

## Faith, fear, and the call

Many families pray in the first minute, and they should not be told that prayer and action are opposites. Make the call and pray in the same minute. A remedy applied at home while the clock runs is the pattern that arrives too late for the treatments that only hospitals can give. Courage, in this illness, looks like a phone in the hand.

Fear of bad news keeps people in the house until the news is worse. The scan may show a small injury that can be treated, a mimic that can be reversed, or a bleed that needs attention now. None of those outcomes is improved by a night of watching. The only outcome that waiting reliably produces is a narrower set of options.

## What to ask at the first outpatient review

Ask what type of stroke it was, in one sentence. Ask what cause was found, and what is still being looked for. Ask which medicines are for prevention and which, if any, are for a short course. Ask what to do if a dose is vomited or missed. Ask the driving rule in a yes, a no, or a date. Ask which new symptoms are an emergency rather than a message to the clinic.

Bring the blood-pressure readings if you were asked to take them, the strips you are actually taking, and any side effect you have noticed: black stool, breathlessness, a rash, muscle pain, or easy bleeding. Do not arrive with a new list of tablets recommended by a friend. Arrive with questions about the list you were given.

If a neck vessel, an aneurysm, or a malformation has been mentioned, bring those images to the surgical conversation. Ask what “severe” means in your report, what the risk of another stroke is with and without a procedure, and what the risk of the procedure itself is in a person with your heart and your age. A picture of a mild irregularity is not a reason for a neck operation. A severe narrowing after a warning stroke sometimes is. The images and the recent symptoms decide. This article does not.

## Closing the gap between knowledge and the call

People who can recite the signs still wait, because the person in front of them does not look like the poster. They look like a parent who is tired, or a spouse who is irritable, or a colleague who says it is a headache. Believe the sudden one-sided change, not the familiar face. Call 112 or 108. Note the time. Give nothing to eat and no aspirin. Go to a hospital that can scan and treat, not to a clinic that can advise tomorrow.

When the emergency is over, recovery and prevention are slow, specific, and worth doing. Medicines, therapy, blood pressure, and a house that does not invite a fall will matter more than any article, including this one. Use the article for the hour you cannot rehearse twice. Use the doctors who have your scans for every hour after that.

## A card you can keep by the phone

Call 112 or 108 if the face droops, an arm or a leg is suddenly weak, speech breaks, vision drops, balance fails, or a headache is sudden and the worst ever.

Say the address first, then “suspected stroke,” then the time the person was last seen well.

Give nothing to eat or drink. Give no aspirin, no blood thinner, and no extra blood-pressure tablet unless the emergency service tells you to.

Do not drive yourself. Do not stop at a clinic or a scanning centre that cannot treat a stroke.

Go to a hospital that can scan the brain in an emergency. Send one person who knows the medicines and the time of onset.

If the signs fade before you arrive, keep going. A transient attack is still an emergency.

After discharge, take the medicines that were prescribed, do the therapy that was prescribed, and call again for any new one-sided sign, a seizure, a sudden severe headache, or a head injury while on a blood thinner.

Bring the images and the discharge summary to any later planned visit. A neurosurgical opinion is for questions that remain after the emergency, not for the emergency itself.

## Why this has to be simple

In the minute that matters, nobody reads a long article. They remember a face, an arm, a sentence, and a number. Everything else on this page is for the calmer hours: the hospital days, the medicines, the therapy, the fear of a second event, and the questions that deserve a specialist once the scan has been done.

Learn the short version now. Teach it once to the people in your house. When you need the long version, you will have time, and you will have doctors. When you need the short version, you will only have the phone.

NEUROKALP remains available for a planned opinion on the brain and spine conditions that follow, or that sit beside, a stroke that has already been taken to hospital. If the stroke is happening as you read this, stop reading and call.

## Scenes that should already be decided

If a parent drops a cup at breakfast and one side of the mouth is lower than it was at dawn, the decision is the call. It is not a discussion about whether they slept badly.

If a colleague’s words stop making sense in the middle of a sentence, the decision is the call. It is not a glass of water and a ten-minute wait in the conference room.

If a headache arrives at full force in a second, unlike any headache that person has had, the decision is the call. It is not a darkened room and a painkiller, however familiar that routine is for migraine.

If the signs were there at dawn and have faded by the time the family gathers, the decision is still the hospital. A deficit that left is a deficit that happened.

If you are pregnant, or weeks from delivery, and a sudden headache, a seizure, or a weak side appears, the decision is the emergency department, where obstetric and stroke care can meet. It is not a wait for the next antenatal date.

If the person is on a blood thinner and falls and strikes the head, even without a new weakness, the decision is a medical check. Bleeding into the head can declare itself after the fall, not only during it.

If you live alone and the signs are yours, the decision is to call before you lie down “for a minute.” Minutes are the treatment window. A locked door and a phone just out of reach have cost people that window.

## After you have done the right thing

You may feel foolish if the scan is clear. You are not foolish. A mimic that has been proved is a good outcome, and the only way to prove it was to go. Emergency teams expect these calls. They would rather see a person whose sugar was low, or whose migraine was severe, than meet them tomorrow with a completed stroke.

You may feel angry if a treatment you had read about was not offered. Ask, the next day, what the clock and the scan showed. Some people arrive too late, some have a bleed, some have a clot too small or a risk too high. Anger that asks for the reason is useful. Anger that delays rehabilitation is not.

You may feel grief when the arm does not return on the timetable you hoped for. Grief is a truthful response. It can sit beside therapy. Tell the team if grief has become a wish not to eat, not to move, or not to be here. That change is part of medical care.

Keep the follow-up even when the person looks better than the neighbours expected. The second stroke is prevented, when it is prevented, by the cause being treated, not by the appearance of recovery. Appearance is a poor guide. The plan written at discharge is a better one.

And if you are reading this in a calm hour, teach the signs to one other person before the day ends. Face, arm, speech, time. Vision and balance. The worst sudden headache. The numbers 112 and 108. That lesson is the part of this page that can be used when nobody has the patience to read the rest.
